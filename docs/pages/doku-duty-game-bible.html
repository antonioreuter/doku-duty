Territory of the Frontier · Bureau of Numbers

Doku Duty

You are a frontier deputy who files numbers for a living. Solve sudoku, rise from Greenhorn to Legend, and try to stop the outlaw Ace from tallying your mistakes on the jailhouse wall.

Narrative

A territory where the law is administered through paperwork

Dusty towns, a self-important bureaucracy, and everyone takes numbers extremely seriously. The comedy is deadpan bureaucracy as a foundation — never the joke itself.

Sudoku puzzles are mathematically identical in every app on the store. There is no puzzle-quality advantage available to win, and every serious competitor is calm, minimal, and blue. Nobody owns comedy in this category. That is the whole thesis: the art direction and comedy writing are the product, and the sudoku engine is table stakes.

The Sheriff

Your boss

Grizzled. Dispenses dry, grudging approval and very little of it. Employs you; does not admire you.

Ace

Antagonist

The outlaw. Tallies your mistakes on the jailhouse wall with weary satisfaction, and cheats at numbers. Never openly hostile, which is worse.

Biscuit

Ally

A burro. Wordless, loyal, visibly delighted by your successes. The only warmth in a cast otherwise made of obstacles.

Number Four

Wildcard

A digit with opinions and a bounty on its head. Occasionally objects to placement and must be coaxed. Establishes that the numbers are people.

Biscuit never speaks. That is a costed decision, not a whim: zero localisation spend across fifteen-plus languages, identical comic timing in every market, and wordless characters reliably become the most-loved in a cast.

The running gags carry the comedy

These matter more than the character art, because they fire constantly and cost little per season.

  • The jailhouse tally. Errors accumulate as scratch marks on Ace's wall, visible on the map and growing across the season. Solve with no mistakes and Biscuit kicks the wall, knocking marks off. Purely cosmetic — it never touches difficulty, rewards, or progression.
  • Digits react to each other. Place a 7 where an existing 7 can see it and both notice, in horror, for about half a second. This is the duplicate feedback — it replaces the conventional red flash.
  • Telegrams as loading screens. Genuine-looking notices — a stolen ledger, mandatory numeracy retraining, a stern reminder about hitching posts. One line each, from a pool of several hundred.
  • The wanted-poster wall. The trophy cabinet is a wall of posters of your own deputy, one per season, in progressively more deranged outfits as rank rises.
  • Ad breaks in character. The Sheriff announces the interruption as a mandatory territorial sponsorship obligation, visibly resenting it.

Rules

How the grid actually works

Standard 9×9. Every puzzle has exactly one solution and is solvable by logic alone at every rank — no guess-and-check, ever.

A cell holds a value or notes, never both

Values are committed answers. Notes are the working out — up to nine candidates per cell. The critical rule is that notes are positional: every digit owns a fixed slot in a 3×3 arrangement inside the cell, and adding or removing one never moves the others.

Two inks, and a one-tap escape hatch

Settled notes are candidates the player has derived. Provisional notes are a hypothesis under test, for chain reasoning at the top tiers. A single control clears every provisional note, so a failed line of reasoning is abandoned in one tap without disturbing settled work.

The note behaviours that make it trustworthy

  • Auto-cleanup, always on. Placing a value removes that candidate from its row, column, and block. Without it, notes go stale within twenty moves and players stop trusting them.
  • Candidate highlighting. Tapping a pad digit highlights every cell holding it as a note — this is how pointing pairs and X-wings become visible rather than deduced by squinting.
  • Auto-notes. Fills every empty cell with its legal candidates. Free during tutorials, then metered; unlimited for subscribers.
  • Notes never count as mistakes, even when wrong.
  • Undo covers notes at full depth, including auto-notes and auto-cleanup as single reversible operations.
The non-punitive rule

Auto-check is off by default and there is no mistake limit by default. Mistakes are recorded silently, for statistics only. The timer is display-only, hideable, and can never fail a puzzle. Overwriting an already-correct cell never counts as a mistake in any mode.

Strict mode exists for players who want stakes — immediate error flagging, three mistakes ends the attempt — and is never enabled without a deliberate choice.

Objectives

Six ranks, graded by thinking — not by clue count

Difficulty is the hardest technique a puzzle requires, so progression feels like getting smarter rather than grinding.

The rank ladder

Marshal+ box-line reduction, naked triple12–20 min
Ranger+ hidden pair, X-wing20–35 min
Legend+ swordfish, XY-wing, simple chains35 min+

Each tier introduces its technique with a short interactive tutorial the first time the player meets a puzzle needing it — skippable, and replayable from the cabinet. Notes become effectively mandatory from Marshal upward, so the notes tutorial must land no later than Sheriff rank.

Three progression layers, on three different clocks

They run independently. That independence is the point: missing a week damages none of them permanently.

Stamps and perks

The non-punitive rule removed lives, energy, timers, and failure states — and with them every source of tension the category normally relies on. Stamps restore a decision without putting anything at risk: is this puzzle worth a Stamp?

Stamps are earned only through play (D-66). They cannot be purchased at any price — no coin store, no bundles.

Hints are metered (D-67). Every puzzle includes one free hint; beyond that a hint costs Stamps or a rewarded video. Subscribers are unlimited.

No puzzle becomes unsolvable, no node locks, no attempt ends. A zero-Stamp player keeps the full grid, the complete notes system, one hint per puzzle, and rewarded video if they want more. Scarcity in the assists is legitimate; scarcity in play is what the ten constraints forbid.

Digit sweep is not candidate highlighting

Highlighting (D-13) is free, always on, and shows where a digit is already marked or placed. Digit sweep reveals where it belongs — solving rather than surfacing. The free feature is never metered.

Because each season brings a rule variant — killer, thermo, sandwich, jigsaw — rather than only a new palette, the map has no content wall. Variants are the most under-exploited content lever in the category.

Mechanism

Feel is engineering, not concept

This is where clones fail. It cannot be added at the end, so it gets budgeted at the start.

Sub-100ms input response. No animation ever blocks the next tap. Nothing exceeds 400ms except one documented exception. Everything is skippable by tap, and OS reduce-motion disables all of it automatically.

Notes trigger nothing

No particle, no sound, no haptic. A player marking a dense board makes forty entries a minute, and any celebration attached to that becomes noise within seconds. Notes are thinking, and thinking should be quiet.

The cascade is the best moment in ordinary play

When one placement completes two or more units at once, the effects chain rather than fire together — row, then column, then block, at 90ms offsets, each brighter and a tone higher. Biscuit stamps on the third.

Completion tint: one flat level, never additive

A cell in a completed row and column and block looks identical to a cell in just one. Stacking tints turns a nearly finished grid into mud, which is exactly when the board most needs to stay readable. A freshly completed unit holds a warmer highlight for about two seconds, then settles — the celebration and the record are different states. A corner mark always accompanies the tint, because colour is never the sole signal for any state.

Boundaries

What the game refuses to do

No firearms, no gunfire, no weapons

Two reasons, and the second is the harder one. Punishment as an aesthetic undoes the design: sixty-five decisions removed failure states, and a gunshot on a wrong digit puts one back in tone even where it does not exist in mechanics.

And it costs money. Weapons push the age rating from 4+ toward 9+ or 12+, restrict which ad networks will serve the app, and forfeit the family audience — a large share of the daily-sudoku market. Dynamite survives this test because it is a celebration, not a threat.

No ad ever interrupts a grid in progress

Interstitials fire only after a node completes, after the celebration, and after an explicit tap. Capped at one per three nodes, six per session, ninety seconds apart. Never on a chest, a boss, or the daily challenge. No banner is ever visible while a grid is on screen, and ad removal is verified by automated test on every release.

No purchasable currency, no gacha, no character lies

Everything in the core loop is free: all puzzles, all six ranks, the full season map, the daily challenge, the complete notes system, offline play. No character ever gives deliberately wrong advice — a character may be unhelpful in tone, never in content. Hints are correct or absent.

Stamps can never be bought. D-23's argument was against a purchase layer that obscures price; D-66 refines it rather than reversing it. There is no coin store and no bundle. If Stamps ever become purchasable, this boundary falls.

The main risk is stated plainly in the product brief: if the character animation and comedy writing are merely adequate, this is a competent sudoku app with no reason to exist. Budget the art before the engineering.
